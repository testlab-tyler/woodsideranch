Benchmark & Build Brief · August 2026

What the best community association websites do

Twenty-nine HOA, POA and master-planned community websites screened against a seven-part rubric, ranked, and reduced to the eight habits that separate the top from the bottom — written as a build brief for Woodside Ranch.

01 — Method

How these sites were judged

A community website has one job: let a resident finish what they came to do, and let a volunteer board keep it true. Everything in the rubric below serves one of those two. Weights are out of 100.

Broken Top, here in Bend, finished last with roughly 2,400 words of destination marketing ahead of a single link to its HOA. Money buys photography. It does not buy a document library.

03 — The top five

What each one is worth copying for

Two of the top five are within forty minutes of Woodside Ranch. That is not a coincidence — Central Oregon associations deal with the same wildfire, short-term-rental and design-review pressures you do, and their sites are shaped by it.

1

Sunriver Owners Association

sunriverowners.org·~4,200 homes·Granicus·94/100

The most complete resident site in the set, and it isn't close. Five plain top-level items — Owners, Departments, Documents & Forms, Community, and I Want To — sit in a permanently visible bar. A utility strip above them carries Emergency Contact, Staff, Calendar and a Translate control. The homepage runs a live calendar and a dated news column side by side, then drops an "Info Finder" panel that lists every governing document by name — bylaws, rules, reserve plans, the ladder fuels reduction plan, the strategic plan — as plain links, no drilling.

It runs on Granicus, a content system built for city clerks. That is exactly why it works: the software's whole purpose is letting a non-technical staffer publish an agenda, a minute and a notice, forever, without breaking the page.

Steal this"I Want To" as a permanent navigation item, and an Info Finder block that lists governing documents by their real names on the homepage instead of hiding them behind a Documents page.
2

Caldera Springs Owners' Association

calderasprings.com/owners-association·WordPress·92/100

The best single page in the entire study, and the most directly copyable thing here. It carries a filterable document library — Governing Documents, Financial Information, Meeting Minutes, Firewise and Insurance — where every file is stamped with its year and its status. Board minutes are posted through February 2026, and drafts are labelled DRAFT, approved ones APPROVED, with the meeting date in the title. Alongside sit the 2026 budget summary, the reserve study, the current certificate of insurance, the wildfire evacuation checklist and the pavilion rental application.

Above the documents: four named humans — the administrator, the design review coordinator, the general manager, the association's CPA — each with a direct phone number and a live email link, plus the evening security line and the Sheriff's non-emergency number. "Pay Your Assessments Here" is a single button to a hosted FRONTSTEPS portal.

Steal thisThe whole page. A year-tagged, category-filtered document library with draft/approved status on minutes, named staff with direct phone numbers, and one hosted payment button. This is the single best model for Woodside Ranch.
3

Kiawah Island Community Association

kica.us·~4,600 properties·WordPress·87/100

The strongest editorial operation of the group. Announcements are categorised and dated — Events, Amenities, Finance, and a running "Kiawah University" explainer series that teaches owners how the island actually works. One post covers the 2027 budget process while it is still open for input, which is a genuinely rare piece of governance transparency. The events rail lists a community workshop on a draft covenant amendment next to trivia night, which is the right instinct: governance and social life on the same shelf. A "Support Hub" collects every document, login and member detail behind one named front door.

Its one real fault is the reason it placed third rather than first: at full desktop width the entire navigation is collapsed behind a hamburger icon. That is a fashionable choice that costs older users the ability to see what a site contains without clicking.

Steal thisCategorised, dated announcements, and an explainer series that teaches owners how the association works. Do not steal the hidden desktop navigation.
4

Palmetto Dunes POA

pdpoa.org·WordPress + AppFolio·84/100

The clearest demonstration that money should have a front door. Owner Login, Pay Annual Assessment and Pay Citation are all reachable from the top of the site, and the Architectural Review Board has a real section rather than a downloadable form. News is current to the first week of August. The lesson is in the plumbing: the public site is WordPress, the money and the owner accounts live in AppFolio, and the join between them is a link. Nobody built a payment system.

Its weakness is menu sprawl — nested items repeat across menus, and "Pay Citation" appears more than once, which reads as clutter rather than helpfulness.

Steal thisPublic site and money system as two separate products joined by a link. Never build card handling into the community website.
5

The Ridge at Eagle Crest (RECOA)

ridgeowners.org·1,700+ homesites·WordPress·82/100

The best labels in the study, from a volunteer-scale Oregon association on ordinary WordPress. Its navigation includes "I Want To…", and its homepage offers three doors: News, Events, and — the phrase worth the whole trip — "Who To Call." Its architectural review entry is not called ARC; it is called "Change My Home or Landscape." Payment is "Make A Payment." Every label is written in the words a resident would use, and the operating documents library is one click from anywhere.

It is fifth rather than higher because the execution trails the thinking: the homepage leads with several hundred words of Central Oregon tourist copy about kayaking and flight routes before it serves a single resident, the news list renders twice on the page, and the newest item dates to June.

Steal this"Who To Call" as a navigation item, and naming the architectural review process "Change My Home or Landscape" instead of "ARC."
04 — The bottom five

Five ways to get this wrong

Each of these failures is cheap to avoid and expensive to live with. Three of the five belong to communities with higher property values than Woodside Ranch.

16

Broken Top Club — 38/100

brokentop.com·Bend, OR

A gated golf community five miles from you, whose "Our Community" page delivers roughly 2,400 words on Cascade sunsets, lava caves and craft breweries before offering one link labelled "Learn More About The HOA." No documents, no minutes, no calendar, no board roster, no named contact beyond a membership email. The site is selling the neighbourhood to people who don't live there while the people who do live there have nowhere to go.

The failureConfusing marketing the community with serving the community. A resident site that reads like a brochure has quietly decided residents aren't the audience.
15

Rancho Santa Fe Association — 40/100

rsfassociation.org·MembersFirst

One of the wealthiest communities in America. The public homepage is a rotating photo slider, a weather reading, three links to the golf club, tennis club and horse ranch, and a paragraph explaining that everything else requires a login. No documents, no minutes, no calendar, no news, no board roster in the open. A prospective buyer, a title company, an insurer or a resident who has forgotten their password all hit the same wall.

The failureTreating the login as the product and the public site as a lobby. Gating is for the resident directory, not for the CC&Rs.
14

North Rim HOA — 42/100

northrimbend.com·121 one-acre homesites, Awbrey Butte

Your closest structural analogue in the study — a small, high-value Bend association — and a warning. Home, About, Location, Gallery, Contact, and a "For Owners" button that hands off to a property manager's AppFolio portal. No governing documents, no minutes, no architectural guidelines, no calendar. The newest dated content on the site is from April 2024. It is a brochure with a login bolted to the side.

The failureOutsourcing the entire resident relationship to the management company's portal, leaving the association's own site with nothing to say and no reason to be updated.
13

Club Course POA — 45/100

clubcoursepoa.com·Wix

Genuinely useful documents — bylaws, minutes, financials — undermined by neglect. The copyright line reads 2023 while the content runs to 2026. There is no online payment; dues go by mailed cheque. And one top-level navigation item reads "ByLaws - Adopted 5-30-25": a single document promoted into the menu bar, which is what happens when a site is edited by adding rather than by organising.

The failureNavigation that grows by accretion. Every time something matters, it gets added to the menu, and nothing is ever removed or grouped.
12

Sea Pines POA — 49/100

seapinespoa.com·MemberLeap

Thirteen flat top-level navigation items with no grouping — Home, Member Directory, Board, Links, Members Only, Volunteer to be a Board Member, Newsletters, Volunteer for Work Groups, Join The POA, Online Store, Contact Us, Community Standards Committee, Login. The homepage is six unlabelled card stubs. There is no online payment and no architectural review path. An active, well-meaning volunteer organisation is being poorly served by a members-club platform doing an association's job.

The failureChoosing a platform for how cheaply it sets up rather than how cheaply it is maintained, and letting the platform's default structure become the site's structure.
05 — The analysis

Eight habits that separate them

These are the patterns present in nearly every top-five site and absent from nearly every bottom-five one. They are the build principles.

1 · Navigation is shaped like tasks, not like the org chart

The best

Sunriver and RECOA both ship "I Want To…" as a permanent menu. Desert Mountain uses "How Do I…". RECOA offers "Who To Call" and "Change My Home or Landscape."

The worst

Menus named after the organisation: About Us, Our Community, Gallery, Did You Know?, Community Standards Committee. The resident has to translate their problem into the association's vocabulary first.

2 · The document library is the product

The best

Caldera Springs filters by category, stamps every file with a year, and marks minutes DRAFT or APPROVED with the meeting date. Sunriver lists every governing document by name on the homepage.

The worst

No library at all (North Rim, Broken Top), everything behind a login (Rancho Santa Fe), or one lone PDF promoted into the navigation bar (Club Course).

3 · Dated, visible proof of life

The best

Every top-five site carries a dated item from the last eight weeks and a calendar populated forward. Sunriver posted election results and Kiawah opened its 2027 budget process this month.

The worst

A 2023 copyright over 2026 content; newest post from April 2024; or no dates anywhere, which is worse — an undated site can't be caught being stale, and residents stop trusting all of it.

4 · Named humans with direct phone numbers

The best

Caldera Springs publishes the administrator, design review coordinator, general manager and CPA by name, each with a direct line and live email, plus security and Sheriff non-emergency numbers.

The worst

A generic contact form, a single info@ address, or a board page listing titles without people. Residents call the property manager instead, and the board loses visibility into its own community.

5 · Money has a front door — and lives somewhere else

The best

"Pay Your Assessments Here" above the fold, linking out to a hosted portal — FRONTSTEPS at Caldera, AppFolio at Palmetto Dunes. The website never touches a card number.

The worst

Mail a cheque to a CPA's street address, or no payment path mentioned at all. Every dues cycle then costs the treasurer weeks of manual chasing.

6 · Public by default, gated by exception

The best

Governing documents, budgets, reserve studies and minutes sit in the open. Only the resident directory and personal account balances require a login.

The worst

Rancho Santa Fe gates everything, and its public site is consequently worthless to buyers, agents, insurers and locked-out residents alike. Over-gating doesn't protect the community; it hides it.

7 · Emergency and safety information is elevated, not filed

The best

Sunriver puts Emergency Contact in the top utility bar. Desert Mountain makes Safety & Security a top-level item. Caldera lists security and Sheriff lines beside its staff, and files its Firewise certificate and evacuation checklist as governing documents.

The worst

Buried under Resources, or absent. In a wildland-urban interface this is the one page that must be findable by someone panicking on a phone with poor signal.

8 · Boring, durable platforms beat clever ones

The best

The two most functional sites in the study — Sunriver on Granicus, Desert Mountain on CivicPlus — run municipal government content systems, built so a non-technical city clerk can publish agendas and minutes indefinitely. That is precisely an HOA board's job.

The worst

Members-club software (MemberLeap), page builders (Wix), and marketing platforms doing governance work. They set up fast, then quietly make the recurring job harder than the volunteer can sustain.

06 — Baseline

Where woodsideranch.net stands today

Scored on the same rubric: 50/100, mid-pack. Not a bad site — an under-built one, with two genuine assets worth keeping.

What's already right. The domain ranks first for its own name, so discoverability is solved. It's on WordPress, which is the same platform three of the top five use. It has a real document section with bylaws, CC&Rs and meeting minutes. And it has a Fire Safety page covering prevention, public alerting and evacuation routes — for a community bordering the Deschutes National Forest, that page is the most valuable thing on the site and it already exists. Most of the top five would rank it as a top-level item.

What's holding it at 50. The homepage is a written description of the menu — four paragraphs explaining that "Association Info has information about the association" and "Neighbourhood has news and event information." A homepage that explains the navigation instead of doing the work is the clearest single symptom of an under-built site, and it costs points across task completion, findability and freshness at once.

Beyond that: no calendar, no dated news stream, no online dues payment, no architectural review submission path, no public board roster with names and direct contacts, and no evidence of a recent update on the front page. Every one of those is present in all five leaders.

07 — Build brief

What to build for Woodside Ranch

Given the constraints you set: you maintain it technically, the board handles content, and it must support documents, calendar, news, contact, dues payment and forms on day one.

Stay on WordPress and keep the domain. It already ranks, the board already has content in it, and it is what Caldera Springs, Kiawah and RECOA run. The problem is structure, not software — rebuilding the structure is a week of work; migrating platforms is a month and throws away the search ranking.

The homepage becomes six doors

01Pay my duesOne button to a hosted portal. The site never handles a card.
02Change my home or landscapeArchitectural review, named the way residents think about it. Not "ARC."
03Find a documentFilterable library, every file year-stamped, minutes marked draft or approved.
04What's happeningDated announcements and a forward-looking calendar, side by side.
05Who to callBoard members and committee chairs by name, with tappable phone numbers.
06Fire & evacuationYour existing page, promoted to the top level and linked from the header.
Payments

Link out, never build in

Use a hosted portal — PayHOA, FRONTSTEPS or a Stripe payment link — and put a single button on the homepage. This keeps card data, PCI scope and refund disputes entirely off your site and off your plate. It is what both Caldera Springs and Palmetto Dunes do.

Forms

Embedded form builder for architectural review

Cognito Forms or Jotform, embedded. The board can edit the questions themselves without touching WordPress, submissions land in a table they can sort by address and status, and you write no form-handling code.

Calendar

Whatever the board already uses

Embed the Google Calendar they maintain from Gmail. Do not introduce a second calendar that only lives on the website — it will be the first thing to go stale.

Editing

Shrink the editable surface to three actions

This is the whole answer to "boomers must be able to change it." Do not give the board a page builder or a theme customiser. Give them a restricted WordPress role that exposes exactly three things: Post an announcement, Add an event, Upload a document. Structure, navigation and layout stay yours. A board member who can only do three things cannot break the site, and will not be afraid to try.

Accessibility floor

Non-negotiables, set once in the theme

  • 18px minimum body text — this report is set at 18px for the same reason.
  • Navigation always visible on desktop. No hamburger menu above 900px. This is Kiawah's one real mistake.
  • Menus open on click, never on hover. Hover menus are unusable with a tremor or a trackpad.
  • Tap targets 44px minimum, and every phone number a real tel: link.
  • 4.5:1 contrast minimum, and no text laid over photographs.
  • No critical information PDF-only. Meeting dates, board contacts and evacuation routes go on the page as text.
Content discipline

One named person owns the posting

Every dead site in the bottom five died the same way: nobody's job. Assign minutes-posting to a specific board role in the bylaws or standing rules, and put a visible "last updated" date on the documents page so staleness is self-reporting.

One thing to decide before I build

You selected online dues payment but not a member login area. Those usually travel together: showing an owner their balance requires knowing who they are. There are two clean ways to resolve it, and it changes the build. Either keep the public site login-free and hand payment entirely to a hosted portal with its own separate login — simplest, and what Caldera Springs does — or add a light members area on the site for the directory and account balances. Your existing site already has a gated Residents section, so the second path is closer than it looks. Tell me which and I'll spec accordingly.
